# Economics Paper Writing

Help the user produce economics writing that is clear, direct, and organized around one main finding. Follow the conventions of top field journals: the reader should learn the question, the answer, and why to believe it within the first page.

## Before writing

1. Identify the paper's **one central contribution** in a single sentence: "This paper shows that X causes/raises/explains Y, using Z." If the user cannot state it, help them find it before polishing prose.
2. Ask (or infer from the draft) the target audience: general-interest journal, field journal, policy outlet, or thesis committee. Adjust jargon and technical depth accordingly.
3. Note what kind of paper it is — reduced-form empirical, structural, theory, experimental, or descriptive — since the evidence the reader needs differs by type.

## Structure

Use the standard order unless the user has a reason to deviate:

1. **Abstract** (100–150 words): question, method/data, main quantitative result, implication. No citations, no vague promises ("we discuss implications").
2. **Introduction** (3–5 pages). In order:
   - The question and why it matters (one paragraph, concrete — no "since the dawn of time" openers).
   - What this paper does and finds, with the headline number(s), stated in the first two pages.
   - Why the result is credible: identification strategy, data, or key modelling assumption.
   - Contribution relative to the 3–5 closest papers, stated as what this paper adds, not a survey.
   - Optional short roadmap only if the structure is unusual.
3. **Background / institutional setting** — only what the reader needs to follow the identification or model.
4. **Data** or **Model**.
5. **Empirical strategy** (for empirical papers): estimating equation, identifying assumption stated plainly, main threats and how they are addressed.
6. **Results**: main result first, then robustness, heterogeneity, mechanisms. Each subsection opens with its takeaway sentence.
7. **Conclusion**: short. Restate the finding, its limits, and one or two implications. No new results.

Move long proofs, extra robustness tables, and data construction details to appendices.

## Prose rules

- Lead every paragraph with its main claim; supporting detail follows.
- Prefer active voice and concrete subjects: "Minimum wage increases reduce teen employment by 2%" — not "It is found that there is a reduction…".
- Use "we" (or "I" for sole authors) consistently.
- Report magnitudes with economic meaning, not just significance: compare effects to means, standard deviations, or policy-relevant benchmarks. Avoid "significant" when you mean "large".
- One term per concept; define it once and never switch synonyms ("treatment group" stays "treatment group").
- Cut throat-clearing: "It is important to note that", "Interestingly", "In this section we will". 
- Keep notation minimal and introduce each symbol where it is first used.
- Tables and figures must be self-contained: descriptive titles, notes stating sample, specification, standard-error clustering, and significance markers.

## Literature positioning

- Group related work by idea, not by author list.
- State precisely how this paper differs: new data, new identification, different setting, or a result that overturns or reconciles earlier findings.
- Never invent citations. If a reference is needed but unknown, leave a marked placeholder like `[CITE: paper on X]` and tell the user.

## Editing an existing draft

When asked to revise rather than write:

1. Give a short diagnosis first: is the main finding stated early? Is the contribution clear? Is the identification argument convincing on its face?
2. Then make edits, preserving the author's results, numbers, and claims exactly. Do not change estimates, standard errors, or the strength of a causal claim without flagging it.
3. Flag overclaiming (causal language from correlational evidence, external validity beyond the sample) as a comment rather than silently softening it.
4. Return edited text plus a brief list of the substantive changes.

## Referee responses

- Open with a short thank-you and a summary of the main changes.
- Quote each referee point, then respond directly: what was changed and where (section, table, page).
- Be courteous when disagreeing, and back the disagreement with evidence or a new analysis.

## Checklist before handing back

- [ ] Central finding and headline magnitude appear in the abstract and on page 1–2 of the introduction.
- [ ] Identification or key assumption is stated in one plain sentence.
- [ ] Contribution paragraph names the closest papers and the specific difference.
- [ ] No fabricated citations, numbers, or results.
- [ ] Every table/figure referenced in the text, with self-contained notes.
- [ ] Conclusion adds no new results.

## Further reading

For the user's reference: John Cochrane, "Writing Tips for Ph.D. Students"; Deirdre McCloskey, *Economical Writing*; Jesse Shapiro, "Four Steps to an Applied Micro Paper".
